# Chapter 03. Actuator AI Co Design

## 03.01. Why Actuation and AI Must Be Co Designed

![](images/image1.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

![](images/image2.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Physical AI differs fundamentally from digital AI because its decisions must ultimately become physical actions. An AI model may generate an apparently correct command, but the robot can only execute that command through motors, drives, transmissions, joints, wheels, brakes, or other actuators. The physical actuator therefore forms the final link between intelligence and the environment. Its response characteristics, limitations, and uncertainties directly determine whether an AI decision can be realized safely and effectively.

Actuation cannot be treated as a separate downstream implementation detail because every AI action exists within physical constraints. Motor torque, acceleration, maximum velocity, response delay, mechanical inertia, friction, backlash, transmission efficiency, thermal limits, and available electrical power all restrict the space of achievable actions. If an AI policy is designed without these constraints, it may learn actions that appear optimal in computation but cannot actually be executed by the physical platform.

The relationship is also bidirectional. Actuators constrain AI, but the AI architecture can determine what actuator capabilities are necessary. A system requiring precise force regulation may need torque sensing, compliant mechanisms, high-bandwidth drives, or more capable motors. A mobile robot designed for rapid acceleration may require a different drivetrain and power system than one optimized for energy efficiency. Consequently, actuator selection and AI capability should be considered together rather than sequentially.

The action representation used by an AI system is especially important in this co-design process. An AI model may output a desired position, velocity, torque, force, acceleration, trajectory, or higher-level action command. Each representation creates different requirements for the actuator and control hierarchy. Position commands can be handled through lower-level servo loops, while torque or force commands require more direct interaction with the mechanical dynamics. The action space therefore becomes an interface between learned intelligence and physical embodiment.

Control frequency provides another critical connection between AI and actuation. Low-level motor control typically operates at much higher frequencies than high-level AI inference because physical systems can change rapidly even when the surrounding environment appears visually stable. The AI does not necessarily need to compute every motor command directly. Instead, a hierarchical architecture can allow AI to generate relatively slower action targets while deterministic control loops continuously translate them into actuator commands. This separation allows intelligence and physical control to operate at appropriate temporal scales.

Actuator dynamics also influence learning itself. When an AI policy observes that a command produces a delayed, filtered, or saturated response, the resulting transition differs from the idealized action assumed by a purely digital model. The policy must therefore learn the behavior of the embodied system rather than an abstract action space detached from hardware. Accurate actuator models, real-world feedback, and system identification can reduce this mismatch and improve transfer from simulation or training environments to physical robots.

Physical limits must be represented explicitly when AI controls actuators. Every actuator has boundaries involving torque, force, velocity, acceleration, position, temperature, current, and power. Commands exceeding these limits may be clipped, delayed, or rejected by the lower-level controller. If such saturation is ignored during AI development, the learned policy can repeatedly request impossible actions and produce unstable or inefficient behavior. Actuator-aware learning instead treats these limits as part of the feasible action space.

Compliance further demonstrates why mechanical design and AI design cannot be separated. A rigid actuator may provide precise positioning but can transmit large interaction forces, whereas compliant actuation can absorb impacts and adapt to uncertain contact conditions. For manipulation, walking, docking, or human interaction, the AI may need to reason about force and contact rather than position alone. The desired intelligence therefore influences actuator compliance, sensing, control bandwidth, and mechanical architecture.

Energy is another shared design variable. Actuation can consume substantially more energy than computation, particularly during acceleration, climbing, lifting, or continuous high-load operation. An AI policy that maximizes task performance without considering actuator energy may produce unnecessary motion and reduce operating time. Energy-aware action planning can instead exploit motion efficiency, reduce excessive acceleration, select appropriate operating modes, and coordinate actions with the available battery and thermal budget.

Feedback from actuators also provides valuable information for AI. Position, velocity, current, torque, temperature, vibration, and fault signals reveal how the physical system is responding to commands. These signals can become proprioceptive observations for state estimation, anomaly detection, adaptive control, and learning. The actuator is therefore not merely an output device. It can become an information source through which the AI observes the consequences of its own actions.

The most effective architecture is consequently hierarchical and co-designed. High-level AI can reason about tasks, goals, environment state, and future consequences, while intermediate layers transform these decisions into feasible trajectories or action targets. Low-level controllers then execute those targets with deterministic timing and actuator-specific compensation. This organization allows advanced AI to remain flexible while preserving the precision, stability, safety, and responsiveness required by physical control.

Actuation and AI must ultimately be designed as one closed-loop system: perception informs state estimation, AI selects an action, control transforms that action into executable commands, actuators change the physical state, and feedback reveals the resulting outcome. The next AI decision is therefore influenced by the actual behavior of the actuator and the mechanical system. Co-design closes the gap between computational intelligence and physical capability, making the robot\'s intelligence consistent with what its body can actually sense, move, control, and sustain.

## 03.02. Actuator Dynamics as AI Constraints

![](images/image3.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Actuator dynamics define the physical behavior that occurs between an AI command and the resulting motion of a robot. An action produced by an AI model is not executed instantaneously or perfectly; it passes through motors, drives, transmissions, joints, wheels, and mechanical structures before changing the physical state. The resulting response depends on inertia, friction, damping, torque capability, electrical characteristics, latency, and mechanical configuration. These dynamics therefore form part of the environment in which Physical AI operates and must be considered when designing, training, and deploying AI policies.

A useful abstraction is to regard the actuator and mechanical system as part of the state-transition process. Given a current state and an action command, the next physical state is determined not only by the intended command but also by the dynamics of the robot. The same command can produce different outcomes when payload, friction, terrain, temperature, battery condition, or mechanical wear changes. In this sense, actuator dynamics introduce a transformation between commanded action and realized action, and an AI system that ignores this transformation can develop an inaccurate understanding of how its actions affect the world.

Inertia is one of the fundamental constraints because physical objects resist changes in motion. A heavy robot, a large wheel, or a manipulator carrying a substantial payload requires more torque and time to accelerate or decelerate than a lightweight system. An AI policy trained without appropriate inertial effects may request rapid changes that cannot be physically achieved. The mismatch can lead to delayed responses, trajectory errors, excessive control effort, or unstable behavior. AI planning must therefore consider the relationship between desired acceleration, available torque, mass distribution, and the resulting motion.

Friction and damping introduce additional differences between commanded and realized motion. Static friction can create a dead zone in which small commands produce little or no movement, while dynamic friction changes the required force once motion begins. Damping can reduce velocity and dissipate energy, affecting how quickly a system responds and settles after a command. These effects are particularly important for mobile robots, manipulators, and systems operating under changing loads. An AI policy that assumes ideal motion may repeatedly compensate for unmodeled friction and damping, producing inefficient or oscillatory behavior.

Actuator response delay is another important AI constraint. Between the generation of an AI action and the physical response, delays may occur in computation, communication, motor-drive processing, current control, mechanical transmission, and sensor feedback. Even a relatively small delay can become significant when the robot is moving quickly or interacting with a dynamic environment. If an AI model predicts the consequence of an action without accounting for this delay, its internal prediction can become temporally misaligned with reality. Latency must therefore be considered as part of the action-to-state transition.

Bandwidth determines how quickly an actuator can follow changing commands. A motor may theoretically accept a rapidly varying command, but the complete mechanical and control system may not be capable of reproducing that command accurately. High-frequency variations can be filtered, attenuated, or delayed by the drive and mechanical system. Consequently, the AI action frequency should not be confused with the low-level control frequency. High-level AI may operate at a slower rate while deterministic controllers execute the detailed actuator commands at much higher frequencies.

Actuator limits also define the feasible action space available to AI. Maximum torque, force, velocity, acceleration, current, temperature, travel range, and power consumption place boundaries on what the robot can physically do. When a policy produces a command outside these boundaries, the controller may saturate the command, clip it, or enter a protective mode. If saturation is not represented during training, the AI may repeatedly select actions that are impossible to execute. Actuator-aware AI instead learns or plans within a feasible region that reflects the actual capabilities of the physical system.

The relationship becomes more complex when compliance, backlash, and contact dynamics are included. A compliant actuator may deform under load and produce a different motion from a rigid actuator, while mechanical backlash can create a difference between commanded and actual position depending on the direction of motion. During contact with an object or terrain, forces can change rapidly and create additional dynamics that are difficult to represent with simple models. These characteristics affect manipulation, walking, docking, obstacle traversal, and other tasks in which physical interaction is central to successful behavior.

Actuator dynamics also create uncertainty that should be represented in learning and prediction. Two robots with the same nominal specifications can behave differently because of manufacturing tolerances, wear, temperature, lubrication, payload, battery voltage, or component aging. A world model or policy that assumes a single deterministic actuator response may therefore fail when deployed under different operating conditions. Training with variations in physical parameters, system identification, adaptive estimation, and uncertainty-aware prediction can help the AI recognize and compensate for these differences.

The effect of actuator dynamics extends into the simulation-to-real transfer problem. Simulation can reproduce idealized or approximate actuator behavior, but real systems contain delays, friction, backlash, saturation, compliance, noise, and hardware imperfections. If these characteristics are omitted from simulation, a policy may learn actions that perform well in the simulated environment but fail on the physical robot. Dynamics randomization and more accurate actuator models can expose the policy to a broader range of physical responses, improving robustness when the learned behavior is transferred to real hardware.

Actuator dynamics should therefore be incorporated into the complete Physical AI control hierarchy rather than handled only at the lowest control layer. High-level AI determines goals and actions, intermediate planning converts them into feasible trajectories or commands, and low-level controllers compensate for actuator-specific dynamics while maintaining stability. Feedback from encoders, current sensors, torque sensors, temperature sensors, and other proprioceptive sources provides information about the actual actuator response. This feedback can support state estimation, adaptive control, anomaly detection, and continual learning.

The central principle is that an AI action should be defined not only by what the model intends to command, but also by what the physical system can actually realize. Actuator dynamics determine the mapping between intention and execution, while the resulting physical response determines the next state observed by the AI. Treating inertia, friction, damping, latency, bandwidth, compliance, saturation, and physical limits as integral parts of the AI problem produces policies that are more executable, stable, efficient, and transferable. Actuator-aware design therefore closes the gap between abstract decision-making and real physical behavior, making the intelligence of a Physical AI system consistent with the dynamics of its body.

## 03.03. Motor Drive and Control Hierarchy

![](images/image4.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

![](images/image5.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

A Physical AI system cannot directly apply an AI decision to a motor because the command must pass through a structured control hierarchy that converts an abstract action into a physically executable response. The motor drive forms the essential interface between software and electromechanical hardware, translating control commands into electrical current, voltage, torque, or motion. This hierarchy separates intelligent decision-making from fast deterministic control while maintaining a continuous feedback path from the physical system back to the AI.

At the actuator level, the motor drive manages the electrical energy required to produce controlled mechanical output. Depending on the motor technology, this may involve current regulation, commutation, pulse-width modulation, inverter control, or other drive functions. The drive must respond to commands while respecting electrical and thermal limits. It therefore acts not simply as a power amplifier, but as a real-time control layer that determines how accurately and safely the commanded motor behavior can be realized by the physical system.

A useful control hierarchy separates high-level intelligence, intermediate motion generation, and low-level motor regulation. High-level AI determines goals, selects actions, and reasons about the environment. Intermediate control converts those decisions into feasible trajectories, velocity targets, force targets, or other executable references. Low-level control then tracks these references at much higher frequency, compensating for motor dynamics, disturbances, friction, load changes, and other physical effects. This separation allows each layer to operate at an appropriate temporal scale.

The high-level AI layer is concerned primarily with what the robot should do rather than with the electrical details of how a motor should be driven. It may select a target velocity, trajectory, steering action, joint configuration, or force-related behavior based on task objectives and environmental conditions. Because this layer operates at a relatively slower rate, it can devote computational resources to perception, reasoning, prediction, planning, and decision-making. Its outputs must nevertheless remain compatible with the capabilities and constraints of the lower control layers.

The intermediate control layer provides the critical translation between AI decisions and actuator execution. A high-level action such as "move forward quickly" is not itself a motor command. It must be transformed into a feasible velocity or trajectory while considering acceleration limits, available traction, vehicle dynamics, payload, and environmental conditions. For manipulators, a desired end-effector trajectory may need to become joint-level references. This layer therefore provides a physical feasibility boundary between abstract AI behavior and detailed actuator control.

Low-level motor control operates on a much faster time scale because electrical and mechanical dynamics can change rapidly. It may regulate current, torque, velocity, or position using feedback from encoders, current sensors, Hall sensors, torque sensors, or other measurements. Controllers such as PID regulators, state-space control, or more specialized servo algorithms can continuously reduce tracking error. The objective is not to make low-level control intelligent in the same sense as an AI planner, but to provide stable, deterministic, and responsive execution of higher-level commands.

The hierarchy also reflects an important distinction between control frequency and AI action frequency. High-level AI does not normally need to calculate every electrical switching event or motor-control update. Instead, it can generate commands at a lower rate while the motor controller executes those commands at a much higher rate. This allows a robot to maintain stable motion even when AI inference is temporarily delayed. It also prevents computationally intensive AI workloads from directly determining the timing of safety-critical motor-control operations.

Motor drive selection must therefore be considered together with the intended AI behavior and robot task. Continuous torque and peak torque requirements depend on vehicle mass, payload, wheel diameter, gear ratio, desired speed, rolling resistance, slope, and duty cycle. A motor that can deliver the required peak torque for a short period may still overheat during continuous operation. The drive, motor, transmission, battery, cooling system, and AI workload must consequently be evaluated as a connected system rather than as independent components.

Different control modes also create different interfaces for AI. Position control is appropriate when the desired physical location must be accurately followed, while velocity control is useful for mobile platforms and continuous motion. Torque and force control become more important when the robot must interact physically with objects or uncertain environments. The choice of interface affects the action space available to the AI and determines what information must be provided by sensors and feedback systems. Thus, motor-control architecture directly influences how an AI policy should represent actions.

Feedback closes the hierarchy. The motor drive does not simply receive a command and produce an output; it continuously measures the actual response and adjusts the control signal. Position error, velocity error, current, torque, temperature, vibration, and fault information can all contribute to this process. At higher levels, selected feedback signals can also be returned to the AI system for state estimation, adaptation, anomaly detection, and learning. This creates a nested closed-loop structure in which deterministic control stabilizes physical motion while AI learns from the consequences of that motion.

The hierarchy must also provide fault containment and graceful degradation. If an AI model becomes unavailable, experiences excessive latency, or produces an invalid command, the low-level control system should still be capable of maintaining a safe state or executing an appropriate fallback behavior. Similarly, motor-drive faults, sensor failures, overheating, excessive current, or mechanical overload should not propagate directly into uncontrolled AI actions. Safety limits, command validation, watchdog mechanisms, saturation handling, and independent protective functions are therefore important elements of the control hierarchy.

A well-designed hierarchy also improves modularity and scalability. The same high-level AI policy can potentially operate across different robot platforms when its outputs are expressed through an appropriate intermediate action interface. Platform-specific motor drives and low-level controllers can then translate those commands according to the characteristics of each embodiment. This separation supports cross-platform development while preserving the hardware-specific control mechanisms required for each motor, drivetrain, manipulator, or actuator configuration.

The central design principle is that motor drives and control layers should be treated as part of the Physical AI architecture rather than as isolated hardware implementation details. AI determines goals and actions, intermediate control determines how those actions can be realized, and low-level motor control executes them with deterministic timing and feedback. The physical response then becomes new information for the system. By maintaining this hierarchy and closing the feedback loop, Physical AI can combine high-level intelligence with precise, stable, safe, and physically executable motion.

## 03.04. Position Velocity Torque and Force Control

![](images/image6.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Position, velocity, torque, and force control represent four important ways of defining how a Physical AI system commands and regulates physical motion. They differ in what variable is treated as the primary control objective and therefore create different interfaces between AI decisions and actuators. Position control emphasizes where the system should be, velocity control emphasizes how fast it should move, while torque and force control emphasize the physical effort applied to produce motion or interaction. Selecting the appropriate control mode is therefore part of AI--actuator co-design.

Position control commands an actuator or joint toward a desired physical location. A controller compares the desired position with the measured position and continuously reduces the resulting tracking error. This approach is effective when geometric accuracy and repeatability are the primary requirements, such as robotic joint positioning, docking, manipulation, or precise placement. For Physical AI, however, a position command should still respect velocity, acceleration, torque, mechanical travel, and collision constraints. The AI can specify the desired state while the lower-level controller provides stable execution.

Velocity control defines the desired rate of motion rather than a specific final position. This is particularly useful for mobile robots because forward speed, rotational speed, and steering-related velocity can be directly connected to navigation and motion planning. An AI policy can therefore select a velocity command according to terrain, obstacles, predicted trajectories, or task requirements while the lower-level controller maintains the requested motion. Velocity control also provides a useful intermediate interface because it allows AI decisions to remain relatively simple while the motor system handles detailed current and torque regulation.

Torque control changes the control objective from motion to the mechanical effort required to produce that motion. The controller attempts to generate a desired motor or joint torque while accounting for the relationship between current, motor characteristics, transmission, and mechanical load. Torque control becomes important when the robot must respond to changing loads or physical interactions rather than simply follow a geometric trajectory. For AI systems, torque can provide a more direct representation of the physical consequences of an action, but it also requires accurate dynamics, appropriate sensing, and careful handling of actuator limits.

Force control extends this concept to interaction with the external environment. Instead of controlling only the internal motor torque, the system regulates the force applied at a contact point or through an end effector. This is important for tasks such as grasping, pushing, assembly, surface following, docking, and human--robot interaction. The desired force must be interpreted together with contact conditions, compliance, friction, and environmental uncertainty. A force-aware AI therefore needs an action representation and feedback structure capable of reasoning about physical interaction rather than position alone.

These control modes can be understood as different interfaces between AI action space and the physical actuator hierarchy. A high-level policy may choose position, velocity, torque, force, or a higher-level trajectory depending on the task. The selected command is then transformed by intermediate planning and low-level control into executable actuator signals. Position and velocity commands often provide a relatively abstract interface, while torque and force commands expose more of the physical dynamics to the AI. The appropriate abstraction depends on how much physical detail the AI must reason about.

The four modes are not necessarily mutually exclusive. A practical Physical AI system can use nested control loops in which position control generates a velocity reference, velocity control generates a torque reference, and torque or current control operates at the lowest level. This hierarchy allows each controller to operate at an appropriate frequency and provides a structured path from high-level AI decisions to electrical motor commands. The AI does not need to replace conventional control; instead, it can determine targets while established feedback controllers provide fast, stable, and deterministic regulation.

Feedback is essential for all four control modes. Position and velocity require measurements from encoders or other motion sensors, while torque and force control may require motor-current estimation, torque sensors, force-torque sensors, or other proprioceptive measurements. Temperature, vibration, current, and fault information can provide additional constraints and diagnostic information. The controller continuously compares the desired variable with the measured physical response and adjusts the command. These measurements can also be exposed to the AI for state estimation, adaptation, anomaly detection, and learning.

The choice of control variable also affects the required actuator and sensor architecture. High-precision position control may require accurate encoders, stiff transmissions, and low mechanical backlash. High-performance velocity control requires sufficient motor bandwidth and predictable drivetrain dynamics. Torque control requires reliable torque estimation and sufficient current-control bandwidth, while force control may require force sensing and compliant mechanical structures. Consequently, the desired AI behavior can influence motor selection, drive electronics, transmission design, sensing, compute requirements, and mechanical architecture.

Physical constraints must remain inside every control mode. A requested position may require excessive velocity or acceleration, a velocity command may demand more torque than the motor can produce, and a torque command may exceed current or thermal limits. A force command may also become unsafe when contact conditions change unexpectedly. Saturation, rate limiting, trajectory shaping, safety monitoring, and fallback control are therefore necessary to keep AI-generated actions within the feasible operating region. The control interface should represent not only what the AI wants to do but also what the robot can safely execute.

The appropriate control mode also depends strongly on embodiment and task. A wheeled mobile robot may naturally use velocity commands for navigation, while a manipulator may combine position and force control during contact-rich tasks. A quadruped may require coordinated position, velocity, and torque control for balance and locomotion, while a humanoid may require whole-body force and torque regulation. The same high-level AI concept can therefore be implemented through different low-level control interfaces depending on the physical platform. This supports cross-embodiment intelligence while preserving actuator-specific behavior.

The central principle is that position, velocity, torque, and force control should be treated as different levels of physical abstraction rather than competing control techniques. AI determines the action appropriate to the task, intermediate layers convert it into a feasible reference, and low-level controllers execute that reference using high-frequency feedback. The resulting physical state becomes new information for the AI, closing the action--control--actuation--feedback loop. Effective Physical AI therefore depends on selecting the control variable that provides the right balance between intelligence, physical awareness, stability, precision, safety, and computational complexity.

## 03.05. AI Command to Low Level Control

![](images/image7.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

An AI command represents an intended action, but it is not yet a physical actuator command. A Physical AI system must transform the output of a high-level policy into a form that the robot\'s control system can execute. This transformation connects reasoning and decision-making with motors, joints, wheels, and other actuators. The command therefore passes through several layers of representation and control before it becomes physical motion, creating a structured interface between AI intelligence and the body\'s real capabilities.

The first step is to define the action at an appropriate abstraction level. An AI policy may produce a desired position, velocity, torque, force, trajectory, or higher-level command. Such an output expresses what the system intends to accomplish rather than the electrical signals required by a motor drive. The control architecture must interpret this intention and convert it into an executable reference while preserving the task objective. This separation allows AI models to reason at a meaningful level without requiring them to directly manage low-level electrical control.

A high-level command often requires intermediate processing before it can reach a low-level controller. For example, a navigation policy may request forward motion, but the system must convert that intention into feasible velocity, acceleration, steering, or trajectory references. A manipulator policy may specify an end-effector target that must be transformed into joint-level references. These intermediate transformations incorporate robot geometry, dynamics, actuator capabilities, environmental conditions, and safety constraints, ensuring that the AI command remains physically achievable.

The low-level controller provides the final computational transformation before the actuator. It receives a reference such as position, velocity, torque, or force and compares it with measurements from the physical system. The controller then generates the appropriate control signal for the motor drive or actuator. Depending on the architecture, this may involve PID control, model-based control, current regulation, torque regulation, or other servo mechanisms. The objective is to track the reference accurately while rejecting disturbances and maintaining stable physical behavior.

The relationship between AI commands and low-level control is therefore hierarchical rather than direct. High-level AI determines what should happen, intermediate control determines how the desired action can be made feasible, and low-level control determines how the actuator should respond at high speed. This hierarchy allows computationally intensive AI reasoning to operate at a relatively slower rate while deterministic controllers handle rapidly changing motor and mechanical dynamics. The resulting separation improves stability, responsiveness, and fault containment.

Command transformation must also preserve the meaning of the original AI action. An overly aggressive transformation can distort the intended behavior even when the actuator remains within its physical limits. Conversely, a conservative transformation may produce safe but inefficient or ineffective motion. The intermediate layer therefore acts as a semantic and physical bridge between AI intent and actuator execution. It should preserve task-level objectives while adapting the command to velocity limits, acceleration limits, torque capability, mechanical constraints, and environmental conditions.

Feedback is essential because the command-to-control transformation cannot be based only on an assumed actuator response. Encoders, velocity measurements, current sensors, torque sensors, temperature sensors, and other proprioceptive signals reveal what the physical system is actually doing. The low-level controller uses these measurements to reduce tracking errors and compensate for disturbances. Selected feedback can also return to higher-level AI for state estimation, adaptation, anomaly detection, and learning, creating a closed loop from decision to execution and back to observation.

Physical constraints must be incorporated during command conversion. An AI policy can generate a command that exceeds available torque, velocity, acceleration, current, temperature, travel, or power limits. The control system must therefore validate, constrain, reshape, or saturate commands before they reach the actuator. Safety mechanisms should prevent invalid commands from producing unsafe behavior, while trajectory shaping and rate limiting can transform aggressive commands into feasible ones. The goal is not simply to reject AI commands but to maintain the intended behavior within the robot\'s executable operating region.

The control interface should also account for actuator dynamics and delays. A command does not produce an instantaneous physical response because computation, communication, motor-drive processing, transmission, inertia, friction, damping, and mechanical interaction introduce temporal effects. If these effects are ignored, an AI system may repeatedly issue corrections based on an outdated estimate of the robot\'s response. A well-designed architecture therefore separates high-level action generation from fast control execution and incorporates the physical response characteristics into planning, prediction, and feedback.

Different embodiments require different command transformations. A wheeled mobile robot may transform an AI navigation decision into velocity and steering commands, while a manipulator may convert an end-effector action into joint position, velocity, torque, or force references. A quadruped may translate a locomotion policy into coordinated joint commands, while a humanoid may require whole-body motion and force regulation. The high-level AI can remain conceptually consistent while the intermediate and low-level layers adapt the action to the specific actuator architecture of each platform.

This hierarchy also provides an important boundary between AI computation and real-time control. AI inference may experience variable execution time because of model complexity, memory access, or competing workloads. Low-level motor control, however, often requires predictable timing and continuous execution. Separating these functions allows the robot to maintain stable actuator behavior even when AI inference is delayed or temporarily unavailable. A safe architecture can additionally use command validation, watchdogs, saturation handling, fallback control, and independent protection mechanisms to prevent high-level failures from becoming physical failures.

The complete process can therefore be understood as a closed-loop transformation: AI intent becomes an action representation, intermediate control converts that representation into a feasible reference, low-level control produces actuator commands, the physical system responds, and sensors measure the resulting state. That state becomes feedback for the control system and, when appropriate, for the AI itself. The fundamental principle is that an AI command should not be treated as direct motion. It is an intention that must be translated through a hierarchy capable of preserving its meaning while respecting the dynamics, limits, timing, and safety requirements of the physical body.

## 03.06. Control Frequency and AI Action Frequency

![](images/image8.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Control frequency and AI action frequency are fundamentally different concepts in a Physical AI system. Control frequency describes how often the control system updates actuator commands to maintain stable physical behavior, while AI action frequency describes how often an AI model generates or changes higher-level actions. Because physical dynamics can evolve much faster than reasoning and planning, these frequencies should not normally be identical. A well-designed architecture separates them while maintaining a continuous closed loop between sensing, decision, control, actuation, and feedback.

Low-level control generally operates at the highest frequency because motors, actuators, and mechanical systems can change rapidly. A motor controller may need frequent updates to regulate current, torque, velocity, or position and to reject disturbances. The architecture described for Physical AI places low-level control in a high-frequency domain, with representative control rates reaching hundreds or thousands of updates per second. This deterministic execution provides precise tracking and stability without requiring the AI model itself to calculate every actuator command.

AI action generation can operate at a substantially lower frequency because high-level decisions usually concern goals, trajectories, policies, or changes in behavior rather than individual electrical control cycles. The AI may generate an action, action target, or trajectory and allow the lower-level control hierarchy to execute it continuously. This means that an AI model does not need to react to every small physical variation independently. Instead, fast controllers absorb many short-term disturbances while the AI concentrates on decisions that require perception, prediction, planning, and reasoning.

The separation between frequencies creates a hierarchical timing structure. High-level AI can operate at a relatively low or variable rate, intermediate planning and reference generation can operate at a medium rate, and low-level control can operate at a high deterministic rate. The exact frequencies depend on the robot, actuator dynamics, sensor characteristics, task, and computational platform. The important principle is not a universal numerical frequency but the assignment of each function to a timing scale appropriate to its physical and computational requirements.

A useful example is the relationship between planning and control. A navigation or locomotion policy may produce a velocity, waypoint sequence, or trajectory at a comparatively slow rate. An intermediate controller converts this output into feasible references while considering constraints and current state. A low-level controller then updates motor commands much more rapidly to track those references. The robot therefore continues to move smoothly between AI updates rather than waiting for a new AI decision before every actuator adjustment.

This frequency separation also provides resilience to AI inference variability. Large AI models can have variable execution times because of computation load, memory access, scheduling, or other system activity. If the actuator depended directly on every AI inference result, such variation could introduce undesirable timing jitter into physical motion. A high-rate deterministic controller can instead maintain the previous feasible reference or execute a safe fallback behavior until a new AI command becomes available. This separation is particularly important for safety-critical physical systems.

The timing relationship must nevertheless be coordinated through an end-to-end latency budget. Physical AI involves a chain from sensing to perception, state estimation, planning, control, actuation, and physical response. Delays can accumulate across these stages, and communication or computation jitter can change the effective timing of the loop. The system should therefore define how quickly information must travel through the complete path and how frequently each layer needs to update. Frequency design is consequently inseparable from latency, determinism, and real-time requirements.

AI action frequency should also reflect the dynamics of the task. A slowly changing environment may not require frequent changes in high-level actions, whereas fast locomotion, manipulation, collision avoidance, or unstable physical interaction may require more frequent policy updates. However, increasing AI frequency is not automatically the correct solution. If the low-level controller already handles fast dynamics effectively, excessive AI updates may increase computational load without improving behavior. The objective is to match the action frequency to the rate at which meaningful decisions actually change.

The distinction between AI action frequency and control frequency also affects hardware allocation. High-frequency deterministic control is naturally suited to MCU or real-time controller hardware, while AI inference and computationally intensive perception or reasoning can use CPU, GPU, NPU, or other accelerators. Intermediate planning and state estimation can occupy an appropriate real-time or near-real-time domain. This partitioning reduces unnecessary data movement and prevents compute-intensive AI workloads from interfering with safety-critical actuator timing.

The action interface should preserve the timing information associated with each command. A trajectory, velocity reference, waypoint sequence, or action target is not merely a numerical value; it also has an intended update rate, validity period, priority, and temporal context. The control system must know whether a command is new, delayed, stale, or still valid. Maintaining timestamps, sequence information, and execution status allows the robot to distinguish normal AI behavior from communication delay or inference failure.

A closed-loop Physical AI system therefore operates across multiple nested time scales. Sensors observe the physical state, AI interprets and decides, planning generates feasible references, high-rate control executes them, and actuators change the physical system. Feedback then returns information to the appropriate layer. The faster inner loops stabilize physical behavior, while slower outer loops adjust goals and actions. This structure allows intelligence to operate efficiently without sacrificing the real-time responsiveness required by physical interaction.

The central principle is that AI should decide at the rate required for meaningful behavioral change, while control should execute at the rate required by physical dynamics. AI action frequency and control frequency should therefore be co-designed rather than forced into a single rate. Proper frequency separation reduces latency and jitter, improves stability and safety, supports efficient compute allocation, and allows complex AI reasoning to coexist with deterministic real-time control. In Physical AI, the right timing architecture is as important as the right model, actuator, and control algorithm.

## 03.07. Action Space and Actuator Architecture

![](images/image9.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Action space defines the set of actions that a Physical AI system is allowed and able to produce, while actuator architecture determines how those actions become physical behavior. The relationship is therefore much deeper than a software interface between a policy and a motor controller. An action space must reflect the robot's kinematics, dynamics, actuator capabilities, control hierarchy, physical limits, and task requirements. If the action space is poorly matched to the actuator architecture, the AI may learn actions that are difficult, inefficient, unstable, or impossible to execute.

A useful Physical AI action space can be expressed through position, velocity, torque, force, trajectory, or higher-level action representations. Position and velocity provide relatively abstract interfaces, while torque and force expose more of the underlying physical dynamics. A trajectory can encode both spatial and temporal intent, allowing the controller to determine how the robot should move between states. The appropriate representation depends on how much physical detail the AI must reason about and how much responsibility should remain with the lower-level control hierarchy.

The actuator architecture determines which action representations are practical. A robot with precise position-controlled joints can expose joint-position targets to an AI policy, while a force-capable manipulator may benefit from torque or force-based actions. A mobile robot may naturally use linear and angular velocity as its primary action space. A quadruped may require coordinated joint or torque actions for locomotion and balance. Thus, action-space design should begin with the physical embodiment and actuator structure rather than being selected independently from hardware.

The mapping from action space to physical actuators is normally hierarchical. A high-level AI produces an action or goal, an intermediate layer converts it into feasible references, and low-level controllers transform those references into motor-drive commands. This architecture allows the AI to operate at an appropriate abstraction level while actuator-specific controllers handle fast dynamics, feedback, and disturbance rejection. The action interface therefore becomes a contract between intelligence and physical execution, defining what the AI can request and what the robot can reliably realize.

Actuator capability also determines the dimensionality and structure of the action space. A system with many independently controlled degrees of freedom may expose a large action vector, but a large action space is not necessarily advantageous. Redundant or physically coupled actions can make learning more difficult and reduce sample efficiency. Kinematic relationships, mechanical couplings, and coordinated motion can instead be incorporated into intermediate control or action representations. This can reduce unnecessary degrees of freedom while preserving the behaviors required by the task.

Physical constraints must be embedded into the action space or enforced immediately after action generation. Torque, force, velocity, acceleration, position, temperature, power, travel, and bandwidth limits define the feasible region of actions. If the policy repeatedly generates commands outside this region, the controller must saturate or modify them, creating a difference between the intended and executed actions. Designing the action space around actuator capability can reduce this mismatch and make learning more consistent with real physical behavior.

The control frequency is closely connected to action-space design. AI action generation normally occurs more slowly than low-level actuator control because meaningful decisions change more slowly than motor dynamics. A high-level policy can therefore generate velocity targets, trajectories, or action sequences while fast control loops execute them continuously. The action space should include enough temporal structure to indicate how a command should be interpreted, while the low-level hierarchy maintains stability and responsiveness at a much higher frequency.

Actuator architecture also influences sensing and feedback requirements. Position-based actions require accurate position feedback, velocity actions depend on reliable motion estimation, and torque or force actions require suitable current, torque, or force measurements. Temperature, vibration, and fault signals can provide additional information about actuator health and operating conditions. These signals can be used not only for control but also for state estimation, adaptation, anomaly detection, and learning. The action space and observation space should therefore be designed together.

Different robot embodiments require different mappings even when they share a common high-level AI representation. A mobile robot may map a navigation action into velocity and steering references, while a mobile manipulator may divide the action between base motion and arm motion. A quadruped must coordinate multiple actuators to maintain balance and generate locomotion, while a humanoid may require whole-body coordination across many joints. This motivates embodiment-specific action adapters that translate shared AI concepts into platform-specific actuator commands.

Action-space design also affects energy efficiency, safety, and scalability. An action representation that permits unnecessary acceleration or aggressive motion can increase energy consumption and actuator wear. A constrained action space can reduce unsafe commands and simplify validation. At the same time, standardized higher-level action interfaces can allow the same AI model or policy to operate across multiple platforms, provided that each embodiment has an appropriate adapter for its kinematics, dynamics, sensors, actuators, and control hierarchy.

The central principle is that action space and actuator architecture should be co-designed as one system. AI should not define an abstract action space first and expect hardware to accommodate it afterward. Instead, task requirements, embodiment, actuator capability, control frequency, physical constraints, sensing, energy, and safety should jointly determine the action interface. The resulting architecture creates a feasible path from AI intent to physical execution and from actuator feedback back to learning. This co-design approach produces intelligence that is not merely capable of generating actions, but capable of generating actions that the robot can actually execute.

## 03.08. Actuator Saturation and Physical Limits

![](images/image10.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Actuator saturation occurs when a physical actuator cannot produce the command requested by an AI system. The requested action may specify a torque, force, velocity, acceleration, position, or rate of change that exceeds the actuator's available capability. Instead of producing the intended output, the actuator reaches a physical boundary and clips or limits the response. Saturation therefore creates a direct difference between AI intent and physical execution, making actuator limits an essential part of the action space and control architecture of Physical AI.

Typical actuator limits include maximum torque, force, velocity, acceleration, current, position, temperature, power, and mechanical travel. These limits are not independent because one constraint can affect another. For example, high torque can increase current and temperature, while sustained acceleration can consume significant power and thermal capacity. A robot carrying a heavier payload may reach its torque or acceleration boundary much earlier than the same robot operating without a payload. Physical AI must therefore treat the feasible operating region as a multidimensional constraint rather than as a single maximum value.

Saturation changes the relationship between a commanded action and the resulting physical state. If an AI policy requests a torque above the available motor capability, the controller may limit the command to the maximum allowable torque. The robot then follows a different trajectory from the one predicted by the policy. Similar effects occur when velocity, current, acceleration, temperature, or travel limits are reached. The realized action is consequently not simply the action selected by the AI, but the result of that action after passing through the physical constraints of the actuator and control system.

This distinction is especially important for learning and planning. If saturation is ignored during training, an AI policy may repeatedly generate infeasible commands because the simulated or abstract action space appears larger than the real actuator capability. The controller may continuously clip these commands, producing large tracking errors, inefficient behavior, or oscillation. In severe cases, a policy that performs well in simulation may fail when transferred to a real robot because physical saturation and other actuator nonlinearities were not represented during learning.

A better approach is to incorporate actuator limits directly into the feasible action space. Instead of allowing the AI to freely generate every mathematically possible action, the system can constrain actions according to the actual torque, speed, acceleration, current, position, temperature, and power capabilities of the robot. Intermediate planning can then generate trajectories and references that remain inside these boundaries. This reduces the difference between intended and realized actions and allows the AI to learn behaviors that are physically executable rather than relying on a lower-level controller to correct impossible commands.

Saturation is not always a simple hard boundary. Physical systems may exhibit nonlinear behavior near their limits, including reduced bandwidth, thermal derating, current limiting, protective shutdown, or changes in response characteristics. A motor may deliver high peak torque for a short period but be unable to sustain the same torque continuously because of thermal limitations. Battery voltage and state of charge can also change available actuator power. Consequently, the effective action space can vary with operating conditions, and Physical AI should ideally represent these changing capabilities rather than assuming a fixed actuator envelope.

Rate limits are also important because an actuator may be capable of reaching a target value but unable to change toward it arbitrarily quickly. Acceleration and jerk constraints limit how rapidly velocity or force can change. These constraints affect trajectory generation, especially for mobile robots, manipulators, and legged systems where sudden changes can create instability or excessive mechanical loads. Trajectory shaping and rate limiting can transform an aggressive AI command into a smoother and physically feasible command while preserving the underlying task objective.

Safety requires saturation handling to occur before an unsafe command reaches the physical system. Command validation can check whether an AI-generated action lies within the permitted operating region. If necessary, the system can clip, scale, reshape, delay, or reject the command. Independent protection mechanisms may also monitor current, temperature, speed, position, and mechanical conditions. Watchdogs and fallback controllers provide additional protection when the AI produces invalid commands, becomes unavailable, or experiences excessive latency. Saturation handling is therefore both a performance mechanism and a safety mechanism.

The control hierarchy should determine where different constraints are enforced. High-level AI should understand the major physical capabilities of the robot so that its planning does not routinely request impossible actions. Intermediate planning can incorporate detailed constraints into trajectory generation and optimization. Low-level controllers provide the final enforcement through current limits, torque limits, velocity limits, position limits, rate limiting, and protective functions. This layered approach prevents every physical constraint from having to be represented explicitly inside the AI model while ensuring that no command can bypass the final actuator safety boundary.

Actuator saturation also provides useful information for AI. Frequent saturation can indicate that the policy is operating too aggressively, that the payload has changed, that the terrain is more difficult than expected, or that the robot has entered a degraded operating condition. Current, torque, temperature, vibration, and fault signals can therefore be used as feedback for state estimation, anomaly detection, adaptation, and learning. Instead of treating saturation only as an undesirable failure, the system can interpret it as evidence about the physical state and remaining capability of the robot.

The relationship between saturation and AI should therefore be represented in world models and action-conditioned prediction whenever possible. A predictive model that understands actuator limits can distinguish between an intended action and the state that will actually result after physical constraints are applied. This improves prediction accuracy, planning feasibility, and sim-to-real transfer. The model does not need to reproduce every electrical detail, but it should capture the actuator behaviors that materially affect the transition from command to physical state, including limits, delays, bandwidth, nonlinearities, and changing operating conditions.

The central principle is that actuator saturation is not merely a low-level control problem. It defines the boundary between what the AI wants the robot to do and what the robot is physically capable of doing. Torque, force, velocity, acceleration, position, thermal, power, and travel limits should therefore influence action-space design, planning, control, learning, safety, and world-model prediction. A Physical AI system becomes more stable, efficient, transferable, and reliable when these limits are modeled and respected throughout the hierarchy rather than discovered only after an AI command reaches the actuator.

## 03.09. Compliance and Force Aware AI

![](images/image11.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Compliance is the physical property that allows a robot to yield, deform, or regulate interaction forces instead of behaving as an ideally rigid mechanism. In Physical AI, compliance becomes important whenever the robot contacts objects, terrain, tools, or humans. A rigid position command may be appropriate for free-space motion, but the same command can produce excessive contact force when an object or surface is encountered. Compliance therefore connects mechanical design, actuator behavior, control strategy, and AI decision-making, allowing the robot to respond safely to physical uncertainty rather than treating every interaction as a precise geometric problem.

Force-aware AI extends this concept by allowing the AI system to reason about forces and contact as meaningful parts of the environment state. Instead of observing only position, velocity, or visual appearance, the system can use force, torque, current, deformation, and contact-related signals to determine what is physically happening. This is particularly important for grasping, pushing, insertion, surface following, docking, locomotion, and human interaction. The AI can then select actions based not only on where an object is located, but also on how strongly the robot is interacting with it and how that interaction is changing.

Mechanical compliance can be implemented in different ways, including compliant materials, elastic elements, series elastic actuators, variable-stiffness mechanisms, flexible transmissions, or software-based impedance and admittance control. These approaches change the relationship between commanded motion and resulting force. A compliant system can absorb impacts and tolerate small positioning errors, while a rigid system can provide high positional precision but may transmit larger contact forces. The appropriate level of compliance therefore depends on the task, environment, payload, required precision, and safety objectives rather than being universally maximized.

Impedance control provides an important bridge between motion and force. Instead of commanding only a fixed position, the controller defines how the robot should respond when an external force changes the desired motion. The resulting behavior can be understood through relationships among position, velocity, acceleration, and force, with virtual stiffness and damping shaping the response. This allows the robot to remain positionally controlled while yielding appropriately during contact. For Physical AI, impedance characteristics can become part of the action interface, allowing AI decisions to specify not only where to move but also how strongly or compliantly the robot should interact.

Admittance control provides a complementary approach in which measured external forces are transformed into motion adjustments. When a force sensor detects contact or resistance, the controller can modify the desired position or velocity accordingly. This is useful when the robot must accommodate uncertain surfaces, objects, or human interaction. Force-aware AI can operate above this controller by deciding the desired interaction objective, while the lower-level admittance mechanism provides fast physical adaptation. Such hierarchy prevents the AI from having to compute every contact response while still allowing its decisions to influence the desired interaction behavior.

Force sensing and proprioception are therefore essential components of compliance-aware Physical AI. Useful signals can include joint position, velocity, motor current, estimated torque, direct torque measurements, force-torque sensing, tactile information, temperature, and vibration. These measurements reveal not only what the robot commanded but also what actually happened at the physical interface. A sudden increase in current or contact force, for example, may indicate an unexpected obstacle, object contact, mechanical resistance, or a change in the environment. These signals can be integrated into state estimation and world modeling to make physical interaction observable to the AI.

Compliance also improves robustness to uncertainty. Real environments contain variations in object position, surface geometry, friction, payload, mechanical tolerance, and contact conditions. A perfectly rigid controller may interpret these variations as large errors and continuously generate corrective commands. A compliant controller can instead absorb small discrepancies through controlled physical adaptation. This reduces sensitivity to modeling errors and can make learned policies more transferable across objects and environments. Compliance therefore becomes not merely a safety feature but also a mechanism for improving generalization and sim-to-real robustness.

The action space of force-aware AI should reflect the level of physical interaction required by the task. For free-space navigation, position or velocity commands may be sufficient, while manipulation and contact-rich tasks may require torque, force, impedance, stiffness, damping, or hybrid position-force actions. A policy can therefore operate at different abstraction levels depending on the task. The high-level AI determines the desired behavior, intermediate control converts it into feasible interaction references, and low-level controllers execute those references using fast feedback. This preserves the distinction between intelligence and real-time physical regulation.

Safety is a major reason to incorporate compliance and force awareness into the actuator architecture. Excessive contact forces can damage objects, overload mechanical components, destabilize a robot, or create unsafe human interaction. Force limits, torque limits, collision detection, current monitoring, compliant control, and protective stops can constrain the physical consequences of AI actions. The system should also recognize when contact behavior has exceeded expected conditions and transition to a safe or fallback state. Thus, force awareness provides both information for intelligent decision-making and mechanisms for limiting the physical consequences of incorrect decisions.

Compliance and force awareness also influence actuator and hardware selection. A system requiring precise contact regulation may need torque sensing, force sensors, higher control bandwidth, compliant transmissions, or actuators capable of accurate torque control. These requirements affect motor selection, drive electronics, mechanical stiffness, transmission design, sensing architecture, and computational requirements. Conversely, the available actuator architecture determines what kinds of force-aware behavior the AI can reliably produce. Hardware and AI must therefore be selected together, because the desired interaction capability cannot be separated from the physical mechanisms that generate and measure force.

The benefits extend beyond immediate control performance. A robot that can sense and interpret actuator forces can identify contact events, estimate object properties, detect abnormal resistance, recognize slips, and adapt its behavior. These signals can become training data for learning physical skills and can support online adaptation to changing conditions. Rich actuator feedback can therefore improve sample efficiency by providing information that visual observations alone may not reveal. It also creates a direct connection between action and physical consequence, which is valuable for learning policies and world models that must predict how actions change the real environment.

The central principle is that compliance and force awareness should be treated as integral parts of Physical AI co-design rather than optional actuator features. AI determines interaction goals and selects appropriate actions, while compliant mechanisms, force-aware control, and high-rate feedback determine how those actions are physically realized. The resulting forces and contact responses become observations that can inform the next decision. When compliance, force sensing, control hierarchy, action space, safety mechanisms, and AI learning are designed together, the robot becomes capable of interacting with the physical world more safely, robustly, and intelligently.

## 03.10. Actuator Feedback for Learning

![](images/image12.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Actuator feedback is a fundamental learning signal in Physical AI because every commanded action produces measurable consequences in the robot's physical body. Motor position, velocity, torque, current, force, temperature, and controller error reveal whether an intended action was actually realized. By observing these signals, an AI system can connect abstract action commands with the true mechanical response of its embodiment.

Unlike conventional digital AI, a physical agent cannot assume that an issued command is equivalent to a completed action. Backlash, friction, payload variation, compliance, terrain interaction, battery voltage, and external disturbances can change the resulting motion. Actuator feedback therefore closes the gap between commanded action and executed behavior, allowing learning algorithms to model the physical consequences of their decisions.

A useful learning cycle can be expressed as command, actuation, physical interaction, feedback, state estimation, and model update. The policy generates an action, low-level controllers translate it into actuator references, and sensors measure the resulting response. Differences between expected and observed behavior provide prediction errors that can improve policies, dynamics models, system identification, or adaptive controllers over repeated interactions.

Position and velocity feedback provide direct information about motion execution. When a joint repeatedly reaches a target slowly, overshoots it, or exhibits tracking delay, the learning system can associate these patterns with particular configurations, payloads, or operating conditions. Such observations enable the AI to learn that nominally identical commands may produce different trajectories depending on the current physical state of the robot.

Torque, force, and motor-current feedback provide richer information about physical interaction. Increased torque may indicate a heavy payload, contact with an obstacle, changing terrain resistance, or an unfavorable manipulator configuration. Force feedback can reveal contact establishment, slipping, grasp stability, or excessive interaction forces. These signals allow learned policies to become sensitive not only to motion but also to how difficult or risky that motion is.

Actuator feedback is particularly important for learning internal dynamics models. A model can receive the current robot state and commanded action and predict the next position, velocity, force, or energy state. The actual actuator measurements then become supervision for comparing prediction with reality. Repeated prediction-error correction gradually produces a model that captures friction, delay, saturation, compliance, and other dynamics that may be difficult to describe analytically.

This mechanism naturally supports online system identification. Robot dynamics change when payloads are attached, batteries discharge, joints warm, tires encounter different surfaces, or mechanical components wear. Instead of treating the actuator model as permanently fixed, Physical AI can estimate changing parameters or latent dynamic states from feedback. The learned controller can consequently adapt its actions to the robot's current physical condition.

Actuator feedback also provides valuable signals for reinforcement learning. Rewards based only on task completion may be sparse and may fail to distinguish mechanically desirable actions from aggressive ones. Torque peaks, tracking error, vibration, energy consumption, contact force, and actuator temperature can supplement task rewards. Learning can then favor behaviors that accomplish the objective while reducing stress, instability, unnecessary motion, or energy expenditure.

For manipulation, proprioceptive actuator feedback can complement vision and external sensing. Vision may indicate that a gripper has reached an object, while joint torque and finger force reveal whether meaningful contact occurred. During insertion, pushing, grasping, or tool use, subtle changes in force and motor current may contain information unavailable from cameras. Learning systems can fuse these signals to infer contact state and adjust actions continuously.

For mobile robots, wheel-speed, steering-angle, drive-current, and torque feedback can reveal terrain and traction conditions. A discrepancy between commanded wheel motion and estimated vehicle motion may indicate slip, while rising current without proportional acceleration can indicate increased resistance. These patterns can become learned features for estimating traversability and adapting acceleration, steering, traction control, or route selection.

Feedback should be interpreted together with actuator limits. A large tracking error does not always mean that the controller is poorly tuned; the requested action may exceed available torque, speed, acceleration, or power. Learning algorithms that ignore saturation can incorrectly attribute physically impossible commands to model error. Actuator-aware learning therefore benefits from explicitly representing limits, saturation states, controller modes, and safety constraints.

Timing is equally important because actuator measurements must correspond to the actions that caused them. Communication delay, controller latency, sensor timestamp errors, and asynchronous sampling can create misleading training pairs. A learning system may otherwise associate a response with the wrong command. Accurate timestamps, synchronized state histories, and known control periods are therefore essential when constructing actuator-feedback datasets for Physical AI.

Feedback can also support learning at several temporal scales. Fast loops may use current, torque, position, and velocity measurements for stabilization, while slower AI policies learn patterns across complete motions or tasks. Separating these timescales prevents a high-level neural policy from replacing deterministic control functions unnecessarily. Instead, learning can optimize references, gains, trajectories, action parameters, or strategies while low-level controllers maintain stable execution.

Safety requires that learning from actuator feedback remain bounded by deterministic protection mechanisms. Current limits, torque limits, joint limits, thermal protection, collision monitoring, emergency stops, and certified low-level controllers should remain authoritative even when an adaptive policy is learning. The AI can explore within an admissible action envelope, but actuator protection must prevent exploratory actions from exceeding safe mechanical or electrical operating regions.

Feedback histories can additionally reveal degradation before catastrophic failure. Increasing current for the same motion, growing tracking error, abnormal vibration, temperature rise, or changes in friction estimates may indicate mechanical wear or actuator damage. Learning systems can model normal actuator signatures and identify deviations, connecting control feedback with condition monitoring, predictive maintenance, and runtime assurance within the broader Physical AI architecture.

In simulation-to-real transfer, actuator feedback provides a direct mechanism for identifying where simulated dynamics differ from the physical robot. Real measurements can expose inaccurate assumptions about friction, delay, motor strength, compliance, or contact behavior. These discrepancies can update model parameters, guide domain randomization, or train residual dynamics models, progressively reducing the gap between simulated experience and real-world execution.

Actuator feedback ultimately transforms the robot body into part of the learning process. Actions are not merely outputs from an AI model; they become experiments whose physical consequences generate new information. Through continuous comparison among intended action, predicted response, and measured response, Physical AI can learn increasingly accurate models of its embodiment and environment while improving control effectiveness, efficiency, robustness, and safety.

## 03.11. Energy Aware Action Planning

![](images/image13.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Energy-aware action planning treats energy as an explicit decision variable rather than an unavoidable consequence of robot motion. In Physical AI, every acceleration, turn, lift, grasp, braking event, and computation consumes limited stored energy. A planner should therefore evaluate not only whether an action can accomplish a task, but also how much electrical and mechanical energy it requires and whether sufficient energy remains for subsequent operations.

This perspective is especially important for battery-powered mobile robots, manipulators, quadrupeds, humanoids, and aerial robots. Two action sequences may reach exactly the same goal while producing very different energy consumption. Frequent acceleration, aggressive steering, unnecessary joint motion, high contact forces, or repeated corrections can waste energy even when the resulting trajectory appears geometrically efficient. Energy-aware planning searches for actions that balance task performance with physical efficiency.

Energy consumption originates from several interacting subsystems. Actuators usually represent a major dynamic load, while AI computing, sensing, communication, cooling, and auxiliary electronics contribute additional demand. The planner does not necessarily need to optimize every electrical component directly, but it should understand that available battery energy is shared. Aggressive actuator behavior can reduce the energy margin available for perception, reasoning, communication, and future tasks.

Actuator feedback provides the observations required to estimate the real cost of actions. Motor current, voltage, torque, velocity, temperature, load, and mechanical motion can be combined to estimate instantaneous power and accumulated energy. Rather than relying exclusively on nominal specifications, a Physical AI system can learn how much energy particular actions actually consume under different payloads, terrains, configurations, velocities, and environmental conditions.

An energy model can therefore become part of the robot\'s internal dynamics representation. Given the current state, candidate action, payload, terrain, and actuator condition, the model can predict both the resulting motion and its expected energy cost. Action planning then becomes a multi-objective problem in which progress toward the task is evaluated together with execution time, energy expenditure, stability, mechanical stress, and safety constraints.

For a mobile robot, the shortest path is not always the most energy-efficient path. A shorter route containing steep slopes, rough terrain, frequent stops, tight turns, or high rolling resistance may require more energy than a longer but smoother route. Energy-aware navigation can incorporate predicted traction, terrain difficulty, elevation, payload, speed profiles, and acceleration patterns when evaluating candidate trajectories rather than minimizing geometric distance alone.

Velocity planning is closely connected to energy optimization. Rapid acceleration can generate high peak current and increase drivetrain losses, while unnecessarily high speed may increase resistance and require stronger braking. Conversely, moving too slowly can increase mission duration and extend the operating time of computers, sensors, cooling systems, and other baseline loads. Efficient planning therefore seeks an operating regime that minimizes total mission energy rather than simply minimizing actuator power at each instant.

Manipulators exhibit similar tradeoffs. Joint configuration influences gravity compensation, torque requirements, collision margins, and mechanical efficiency. A robot arm may reach the same object through multiple configurations, but some require substantially greater joint torque or unnecessary movement. Energy-aware manipulation can favor configurations and trajectories that reduce large torque demands while preserving reachability, dexterity, contact quality, and task completion.

Compliance and force-aware control can further reduce unnecessary energy expenditure. Excessive stiffness or contact force may cause actuators to continuously oppose the environment, generating heat without producing useful motion. When the task permits, compliant behavior can allow the robot to exploit physical contact, gravity, momentum, or passive mechanical properties. The resulting strategy combines actuator architecture, control behavior, and AI planning rather than optimizing them independently.

Energy-aware planning must also consider actuator efficiency maps and physical operating regions. Motors and drives do not convert electrical energy into mechanical work with constant efficiency across all speeds and torques. A planner that understands these characteristics can prefer trajectories that keep actuators within efficient operating regions. Hardware-software co-design can consequently shape gear ratios, motor sizing, control policies, and action spaces around the expected task distribution.

Regenerative behavior can alter the energy calculation when the actuator architecture supports it. During deceleration, downhill motion, or controlled lowering of a load, some systems can recover mechanical energy and return electrical energy to the battery or power bus. Planning can exploit these opportunities, but regeneration must respect battery charging limits, drive capabilities, thermal conditions, stability requirements, and safety constraints rather than assuming that all braking energy is recoverable.

Battery state introduces a longer planning horizon. State of charge alone does not completely describe available capability because temperature, aging, discharge rate, internal resistance, and transient power demand can affect usable energy. Energy-aware Physical AI should therefore distinguish remaining energy from immediately available power. A robot may possess enough nominal battery capacity to finish a mission while still being unable to support a required high-power maneuver safely.

This distinction encourages hierarchical planning. A mission planner can allocate energy among tasks, travel, contingency reserves, and return-to-charge requirements, while a local planner optimizes trajectories and velocities. Low-level controllers can regulate torque and acceleration within instantaneous power limits. Coordinating these timescales prevents a high-level policy from selecting a mission whose aggregate energy demand cannot be supported by the physical platform.

Learning can improve these estimates continuously. Recorded relationships among commands, actuator feedback, terrain, payload, battery state, and measured energy consumption can train predictive energy models. As operational experience accumulates, the robot can discover that particular surfaces, payload configurations, maneuvers, or actuator states systematically require more energy than expected. The planner can then adapt future decisions using evidence collected from its own embodiment.

Reinforcement learning can incorporate energy directly into the reward or cost function. Task success may provide positive reward while excessive current, torque peaks, unnecessary motion, or energy consumption introduces penalties. However, the weighting must be chosen carefully because excessive emphasis on energy conservation can produce slow or ineffective behavior. Energy efficiency is therefore usually optimized alongside completion time, robustness, safety, and task quality rather than treated as the sole objective.

Energy constraints become more important during uncertainty and unexpected events. A blocked route, wheel slip, repeated grasp failure, communication outage, or localization problem can consume reserves that were not included in the nominal plan. Robust planning should preserve an energy margin for recovery, replanning, safe stopping, communication, and return to a charging location. Remaining battery energy therefore influences which future actions remain safely achievable.

At the fleet level, energy-aware planning extends from individual actions to operational scheduling. Robots with different battery states, payloads, actuator efficiencies, and locations can be assigned different tasks. Charging can be coordinated with mission demand, and energy-intensive jobs can be allocated to platforms with sufficient reserves. In this way, actuator-level energy models contribute to fleet-level productivity rather than remaining isolated within motor-control software.

Energy-aware action planning ultimately connects intelligence with the physical cost of acting. A capable Physical AI system should understand not only what action will move the world toward a desired state, but also what that action costs its body. By combining actuator feedback, learned dynamics, battery state, physical constraints, and task objectives, the robot can select actions that preserve endurance while maintaining performance, robustness, and safety.

## 03.12. Actuator Aware AI Control Example [w/Code]

![](images/image14.png){width="7.268055555555556in" height="7.268055555555556in"}

Actuator-aware AI control can be illustrated with a mobile manipulator that must approach a workstation, position its arm, grasp an object, and transport it safely. The AI does not treat velocity, joint position, or torque commands as abstract outputs. Instead, each candidate action is evaluated against motor limits, joint dynamics, payload, battery condition, control frequency, and the physical state of the robot before execution.

The control architecture separates high-level AI decisions from deterministic low-level control. Perception and state estimation provide the robot pose, object location, joint configuration, contact state, and environmental context. A learned policy or planner selects desired motion, while the actuator-aware layer converts that intention into feasible velocity, position, torque, or force references that the embedded motor controllers can execute reliably.

Consider the mobile base approaching a manipulation target. The AI planner may initially request a rapid acceleration to reduce travel time, but the actuator-aware controller evaluates available wheel torque, traction, payload mass, and acceleration limits. If the requested motion would cause saturation or excessive slip, the command is reshaped into a feasible acceleration profile rather than being passed directly to the motors.

This distinction prevents the AI from learning an incorrect relationship between command and motion. Without actuator awareness, the policy might repeatedly request acceleration beyond the drivetrain capability and interpret the resulting tracking error as uncertainty in the environment. By exposing saturation states and physical limits to the policy, the system can learn that some actions are infeasible because of embodiment constraints rather than perception or prediction errors.

As the robot reaches the workstation, the planning objective changes from fast transportation to precise positioning. The controller reduces base velocity and acceleration while monitoring wheel speed, motor current, localization error, and stopping behavior. The AI can use previous actuator feedback to estimate the braking distance required for the current payload and surface condition, improving docking accuracy without demanding unrealistically aggressive corrections.

The manipulator introduces additional actuator constraints. A desired end-effector pose may correspond to several possible joint configurations, but these configurations can require different joint torques and produce different proximity to limits or singularities. An actuator-aware planner evaluates the candidate configurations together with torque capability, joint velocity limits, gravity loading, and expected contact forces before selecting the motion.

Suppose the robot must lift an object whose mass is uncertain. Initial motion can be deliberately conservative, allowing joint torque and motor-current feedback to reveal the effective load. If measured torque exceeds the expected value, the system can update its payload estimate and reduce acceleration or select a mechanically favorable configuration. Feedback therefore changes subsequent actions rather than merely recording whether the original command succeeded.

During grasping, the AI may specify a desired contact behavior instead of directly commanding maximum actuator effort. Finger or gripper current, force estimates, position error, and object motion provide evidence about whether contact has occurred and whether the grasp is stable. Once sufficient holding force is detected, additional actuator effort can be avoided, reducing energy consumption, heating, object damage, and unnecessary mechanical stress.

The same principle applies during contact-rich manipulation. If the robot inserts a component or pushes against a surface, small position errors can generate large forces when the mechanism is stiff. Force-aware or compliant control allows the system to modify the commanded trajectory according to measured interaction forces. The AI determines the task objective, while the lower control layers preserve stable and physically acceptable interaction.

A practical actuator-aware action representation can include desired base velocity, steering or wheel references, joint targets, end-effector motion, force objectives, and execution duration. These commands can be accompanied by constraints such as maximum torque, acceleration, contact force, power, or temperature. The action space therefore represents not only what the robot should do but also the physical envelope within which the action should be executed.

Feedback from execution returns position, velocity, torque, current, temperature, tracking error, saturation state, and contact information. These measurements are synchronized with the original command and stored with the estimated robot and environment state. The resulting transition contains the intended action and the physically realized action, which is essential for learning dynamics models that accurately represent the real robot.

A learned dynamics model can predict the next robot state from the current state and candidate action. An actuator-aware version additionally predicts quantities such as tracking error, required torque, energy consumption, slip probability, or saturation risk. The planner can then reject actions that appear geometrically valid but are likely to exceed physical capability, producing trajectories that are executable rather than merely mathematically possible.

This approach naturally supports model predictive control(Model Predictive Control, MPC). Multiple short-horizon action sequences can be simulated using the learned or hybrid dynamics model, and each sequence can be evaluated for task progress, tracking quality, energy, actuator stress, and safety. Only the first feasible action is executed before new feedback updates the state and the optimization is repeated, creating continuous adaptation to physical conditions.

Learning can further improve the controller through residual modeling(Residual Modeling). Conventional equations may describe nominal robot dynamics, while a learned residual estimates effects that are difficult to model precisely, including friction, backlash, tire deformation, payload variation, actuator delay, and temperature-dependent behavior. The combination retains useful physical structure while allowing experience to correct systematic modeling errors.

Energy awareness can be integrated into the same example. When several feasible motions achieve the task, the planner can compare predicted motor currents, joint torques, travel duration, and total energy. A smooth trajectory with moderate acceleration may be preferred over a faster trajectory containing repeated torque peaks. The resulting behavior balances task time against battery endurance, actuator heating, mechanical wear, and available power.

Safety remains outside the authority of the learned policy. Motor drives and embedded controllers enforce current, torque, velocity, position, and thermal limits even if the AI generates an inappropriate command. Collision monitoring and emergency stopping provide additional protection. The AI operates within this constrained envelope and learns to avoid repeatedly requesting actions that deterministic safety mechanisms would reject.

A simplified implementation can therefore be organized as a repeating sensing-to-action loop. The system estimates state, generates candidate actions, predicts their physical consequences, checks actuator and safety constraints, selects a feasible action, executes it through low-level control, and measures the response. Prediction error and actuator feedback then update the state, energy estimate, dynamics model, or policy before the next decision cycle.

The central lesson of this example is that actuator-aware AI control does not replace conventional motor control with a neural network. It connects intelligent planning with the realities of motors, drives, transmissions, joints, wheels, batteries, and physical contact. High-level AI determines useful behavior, low-level controllers provide deterministic execution, and actuator feedback continuously informs learning about what the physical system can actually achieve.

Through this architecture, Physical AI becomes aware of its own embodiment while acting. The robot learns not merely that an action reached a goal, but how much torque, energy, time, tracking error, contact force, and mechanical effort were required. This information enables future actions to become more feasible, efficient, adaptive, and safe, completing the actuator-AI co-design loop described throughout the chapter.
